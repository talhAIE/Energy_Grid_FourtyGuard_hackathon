What Is It?
In one sentence: software that watches the weather and warns the power grid before it
gets overwhelmed by everyone turning on their AC at once.
Think about the hottest day of the year in any city. As the temperature climbs, more and more people
switch on air conditioners at roughly the same time. That sudden jump in electricity use is called a 
demand spike. If a spike is big enough and the power company isn't ready for it, the result is a blackout
— the grid simply can't supply that much power fast enough.
Right now, power companies mostly react to this after it starts happening, or they rely on city-wide
weather forecasts that are too broad to say exactly which neighborhoods will spike first. Our project
closes that gap.
The Idea in Plain Words
We take FortyGuard's hyperlocal temperature data (accurate down to individual city blocks) and
turn it into an early-warning system: "Zone 4 is about to get hot enough that electricity demand
there will spike in the next few hours — here's how much, and here's how long you have to
prepare."
It's not a weather app. It's a decision-support agent — something that watches conditions continuously,
figures out when a real risk is forming, and recommends an action to a human, rather than just displaying
numbers on a screen.

What Problem Does It Solve?
The aim: give power utilities enough advance warning about heat-driven electricity
demand that they can act before things go wrong — not after.
The problem, step by step
Heat drives electricity use. Air conditioning is one of the single biggest drivers of electricity demand
in hot climates — and that demand isn't spread evenly across a city.
Grids have limits. Every power grid can only supply so much electricity at once. When demand
suddenly outpaces supply, utilities either scramble for emergency power or are forced into rolling
blackouts.
Current forecasting is too broad. Most demand planning today uses city-wide or region-wide
weather data. It can't tell a utility "this specific district will spike two hours before that one."
Reaction is slower than prevention. By the time a utility sees demand actually rising on their
systems, they've already lost the best window to act calmly — rerouting power, alerting large
consumers, or bringing backup capacity online.
Why this matters to FortyGuard specifically: Energy Trading is one of only three industries FortyGuard
has publicly named as a 2026 growth focus, alongside Agriculture and Rail. This isn't us guessing at
relevance — it's a problem they've already said they want solved.

How It Actually Works
Five layers, from raw temperature data to a recommendation a human approves.
1 Start with FortyGuard's heat data
We pull current temperature and a 12-hour forecast for each zone we define in a city — this is
FortyGuard's core product, already hyperlocal (accurate to roughly city-block level).
2 Apply "Cooling Degree Days" — a real, proven formula
Utilities already use this concept: every degree the temperature rises above a comfortable baseline
(commonly around 18–20°C / 65°F), electricity use for cooling goes up in a predictable, well-
documented way. This isn't something we're inventing — it's an established method in the energy
industry, which makes our model scientifically defensible rather than a guess.
3 Calibrate with public demand data
We layer in publicly available electricity-demand datasets (for example, open grid-operator data) so
our model isn't just theoretical — it's tuned against how demand has actually behaved during past
heat events.
4 Turn it into a per-zone risk score
For every zone, the system produces a simple 0–100 "Demand Spike Risk" score, updated as new
forecast data comes in — along with a plain-language reason (e.g. "Zone 4: temperature rising fast,
historically correlates with a 22% demand increase within 3 hours").
5 Wrap it in an autonomous agent loop
The system doesn't just calculate once — it continuously runs a six-step loop: Observe conditions → 
Evaluate the risk score → Plan a recommended action (e.g. "pre-position backup capacity in Zone 4")
→ Validate against safety guardrails → Act by sending the recommendation to a human → Record the
decision and outcome for a full audit trail. A human always approves before anything is treated as final
— the AI recommends, it never acts alone.
Why this counts as an "AI Agent" and not just a chart: The hackathon's official brief calls out AI Agents
as a primary track. A dashboard that only displays numbers is a report. Our system continuously
watches, reasons, and recommends action — that's the difference between a display and an agent.
